Ratha Yātrā
## The Journey to Gundichā
@image 2

The Ratha Yātrā, the Festival of Chariots, is one of the best-known festivals of India, celebrated above all at Puri in Odisha. It is the annual journey of Lord Jagannātha, with His elder brother Balabhadra and His sister Subhadrā, from the sanctum of the Jagannātha Temple to the Gundichā Temple, a festival that unites sacred story, devotion, culture and social values in one majestic celebration and draws devotees from across the world.

Its roots lie deep in sacred tradition. It is said that Subhadrā once wished to visit the home of her mother's sister, and to fulfil her wish, Lord Jagannātha and Balabhadra took her to the Gundichā Temple. Every year the Ratha Yātrā celebrates this journey as a divine family reunion, and it also recalls Lord Krishna's childhood at Vrindavan, stirring feelings of love and longing.

In another understanding, the journey is the deities leaving their sacred abode to give darshan to devotees who cannot otherwise enter the temple, above all those from the margins of society. Carried out of the temple in grand chariots, the deities show the Lord reaching out to everyone.

The Ratha Yātrā is the last stage of a cycle that begins with the Snāna Yātrā, the bathing festival on the full moon of Jyeshtha, after which the deities, said to have fallen ill, rest for a fortnight out of public view in the Anavasara. During those days the rites pass to the Daitapatis, servitors believed to descend from the tribal devotee Vishvāvasu, for in their illness the deities are held to be closer to their original form, from before their enshrinement in the temple. While the deities are unseen, devotees receive darshan of painted cloths called the Anasara Paṭṭis, which represent them as they heal, and worshipping these is believed to bring the same blessings.

@image 3

At the end of the Anavasara comes the Netrotsava, also called the Navayauvana Darshan, the sight of the renewed youth. On this day the Lord is held to have recovered fully and to appear in a new, youthful form. The eyes of the deities, symbolically closed during their illness, are repainted in an elaborate ceremony conducted in secrecy, and Lord Jagannātha regains His divine sight, renewing the exchange of glances between the Lord and His devotees. The Netrotsava, which falls on the day before the Ratha Yātrā, also completes the work of the temple artists, and the freshly painted deities stand resplendent, ready for their annual journey.

The morning after the Netrotsava, amid chanting, the blowing of conches and folk performances, the deities are carried in a royal procession to their chariots in the ritual called the Pahandi Bije, and the chariot festival begins in full splendour as the renewed deities set out for their summer retreat at the Gundichā Temple.

The Ratha Yātrā begins on the second day, the dvitīyā, of the bright fortnight of Āshāḍha, which usually falls in June or July, and the festival spans nine days. The day before, the Gundichā Temple is ritually cleansed and made ready to receive the deities in the Gundichā Mārjana. On the day itself, after the Pahandi, the three chariots are pulled by thousands of devotees from the Jagannātha Temple to the Gundichā Temple, a distance of about three kilometres.

Every year new chariots are built from sacred wood brought from particular forests, a vast undertaking carried out by carpenters of hereditary families, and each chariot has its own name, design and meaning. Lord Jagannātha's chariot, the Nandighosha, is the tallest; it has sixteen wheels and red and yellow canopies. Balabhadra's chariot, the Tāladhvaja, has fourteen wheels and a blue and red canopy. Subhadrā's chariot, the Darpadalana, has twelve wheels and a red and black canopy, symbolic of Shakti. Each chariot has its charioteer, its flag, its guardian deities and the side deities known as the Pārshva Devatās, and even the ropes by which the chariots are pulled bear sacred names: the whole structure is filled with ritual meaning.

Beyond its spiritual heart, the Ratha Yātrā is a great cultural celebration. Traditional music, dance, fire-spinning and folk chants fill the streets of Puri, and the Dahukas, the traditional bards, sing the bold yet symbolic verses called the Dahuka Boli, which are considered essential to move the chariots. One of the most touching rituals is the Chhera Pahanra, in which the king of Puri sweeps the chariots with a golden broom, an act of humility that reminds devotees that all are equal in the eyes of God, and a powerful picture of royal dignity bowing in service.

## Hera Panchamī and the Return
Five days into the stay at the Gundichā Temple comes the Hera Panchamī. The Goddess Lakshmi, left behind in the main temple, pays a dramatic visit to the Gundichā Temple and, angered at being left out, demands Lord Jagannātha's return. The ritual includes symbolic gestures such as breaking part of His chariot and her retreat by a secret lane, a divine drama that gives the deities human feelings.

After seven days at the Gundichā Temple the deities begin the Bāhuḍā Yātrā, their return journey to the Jagannātha Temple. On the way they stop at the temple of Mausī Mā, the mother's sister, where they are offered poḍa piṭhā, a traditional sweet believed to be their favourite.

Once back, the deities remain on their chariots for the Sunā Besha, in which they are adorned with more than two hundred kilograms of gold ornaments, a sign of divine majesty and abundance. The final ritual, the Nīlādri Bije, returns the deities to their sanctum and completes the cycle of the festival.

## The Meaning of the Chariots
The Ratha Yātrā is rich in symbolism. The chariots represent the body, the ropes stand for devotion, and the pulling of the chariots is the soul's journey towards God. The festival is also a picture of the Lord leaving His own abode to bless all His devotees, breaking down the barriers of caste, creed and status, and the open sight of the deities affirms that the divine belongs to everyone and is not confined to the sanctum.

The Ratha Yātrā is no longer celebrated at Puri alone. Inspired by it, chariot processions are held across India and in great cities around the world, among them London, New York and Sydney, often organised by spiritual bodies such as ISKCON. This worldwide celebration reflects the universality of Lord Jagannātha's message of love, unity and devotion.
