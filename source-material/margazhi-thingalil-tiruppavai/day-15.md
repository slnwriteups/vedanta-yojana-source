# Day 15: Ellē Iḷaṅkiḷiyē

@verse 15

Āṇḍāl begins with a seemingly casual, even slightly sharp address, “ellē iḷam kiḷiyē,” which the commentators explain as a form of familiar sambodhana that she herself quickly feels is too harsh for a devotee-friend. So she refines it by likening the girl to a young parrot (kiḷi), a disciple who faithfully repeats what the āchārya teaches, unlike the cuckoo that sings its own tune. The girl inside, still in sleep and irritation, responds like a typical friend, “shil enṟaḻaiyēnmin,” do not tease me with such words, implying she does not want to be disturbed. Realizing that her approach may have hurt her friend and the other devotees waiting outside, Āṇḍāl begins to soften, acknowledging the rhetorical strength and debating skill of the girl inside, and the exchange slowly turns into a mutual “blame game” about who actually talks too cleverly.

At this point, Āṇḍāl concludes that if this continues, it will only become endless argument, not awakening. So she cuts through the quarrel with “vallīrgaḷ nīṅgaḷē nānē tān āyiḍuga,” accepting the blame wholly upon herself. Commentators beautifully connect this to Bharata’s stance in Rāmāyaṇa, where he refuses to put the blame on Mantharā, Kaikeyī, Dasharatha, or even Rāma, and finally says the fault is his alone for being born and thereby allowing this calamity to arise. In the same spirit, Āṇḍāl teaches that sometimes for the sake of peace in family, community, or saṅgha, a person must even be ready to say “the fault is mine,” even when technically innocent, in order to heal and move forward.

Once she has taken the entire blame on herself, Āṇḍāl changes the direction of the conversation. If you do not trust my words when I tell you everyone has already left, she says, “ellārum pōndārō pōndār pōndeṇṇikkoḷ,” come out and see for yourself, count them one by one. There is no more argument now, only a gentle, practical invitation to witness what is actually happening. From this softened mood, she redirects the attention to the true purpose of the morning, namely, to praise Krishna, the wondrous Lord, “vallānai konṟānai māṟṟārai māṟṟaḻikka vallānai māyanai,” the One who is capable, who destroys enemies, and who can overturn even those who oppose Him. The focus shifts from “who is at fault among us” to “who is infinitely powerful and merciful among the gods,” that is, Sri Krishna.

In traditional understanding, this fifteenth pāsuram is called “pāvaiyil pāvai” or the gem among the Tiruppāvai verses, because it distills the central ethic of the work: humility, self-accountability, and the choice of surrender over egoistic correctness. It also completes the sequence of waking the bhāgavatas from pāsurams 6 to 15, serving as a Suprabhātam not to Bhagavān but to His devotees. From the next pāsurams onward, Āṇḍāl moves towards the house of Krishna under Nandagopa and Yashodā, carrying this inner transformation with her.
