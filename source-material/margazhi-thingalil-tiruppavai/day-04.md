# Day 4: Āḻi Maḻaikkaṇṇā

@verse 4

This pāsuram takes the prosperity described in the third verse and goes one layer deeper, using the image of rain to speak about both the Lord and the Āchārya. Āṇḍāḷ first connects rainfall with Krishna as the wielder of divine weapons. Bhīṣma, when concluding the Vishṇu Sahasranāma, praises Devakīnandana as the one who bears the Sudarśana cakra, shining like countless suns, the Pāñcajanya śaṅkha, the Śārṅga bow, the Kaumodakī gadā, and the Nandaka sword. In this pāsuram she echoes that vision: the blazing flash of lightning recalls the radiance of the chakram; the rolling sound of thunder mirrors the mighty blast of Pāñcajanya; and the sharp, slanting fall of the raindrops is likened to arrows let loose from Śārṅga. Rain is no longer a mere weather event; it becomes a visible trace of the Lord’s presence and activity.

She then describes how rain itself forms. The sun draws up water from the oceans, even though that water is salty; this becomes vapor, gathers into clouds, and slowly darkens them as they grow heavy with rain. The dark, rain-bearing cloud is compared to the form of the Lord—often celebrated as Karuṇa-megham or Nīla-meghaśyāma—because just as the cloud holds water for the thirsty earth, he holds mercy for the jīvas. When the time is right, that stored-up compassion pours down as gentle rain that people can actually receive and live by.

Many Āchāryas see in this pāsuram a gentle but powerful portrait of the guru. The vast ocean, full of water yet mixed with salt, is likened to the Lord, whose greatness and absolute freedom (svātantrya) are real but far beyond what the jīva can directly take in. The cloud stands for the Āchārya: from that inexhaustible ocean he draws only what will truly nourish the śiṣya, leaving aside the “salt” of what would overwhelm, confuse, or harm.

The rain itself is the guru’s upadeśa and anugraha, the teaching and grace that come down in a form that is sweet, usable, and life-giving. Seen this way, the pāsuram is not merely a prayer for good monsoon and rich harvests; it is a quiet salutation to the guru who stands between the infinite Lord and the limited jīva, making otherwise unreachable compassion “drinkable,” just as a cloud turns ocean-water into cool, fresh rain.
