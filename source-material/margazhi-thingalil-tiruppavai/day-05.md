# Day 5: Māyanai Mannu

@verse 5

This pāsuram is the last of the five introductory pāvai verses. In the first verse, Āṇḍāḷ presents Mārgazhi itself as a sacred, auspicious month chosen for observing the pāvai nōmbu. In the second, she lays down the basic niyamas—the do’s and don’ts that purify the body and mind for this vrata. In the third, she speaks of the saubhāgya and prosperity that a land and its people will enjoy when they align themselves with the Lord and His devotees. In the fourth, she celebrates the blessing of timely, abundant rains, which sustain crops, cows, and all beings. In this fifth verse, Āṇḍāḷ finally turns from the upodghāta (prelude) to the Lord Himself as the direct object of meditation and worship. She first hails Him as māyan—not “illusory,” but the One whose ananta-kalyāṇa-guṇas and līlās are so astonishing that they endlessly amaze the jīva. The term here does not denote māyā as delusion; rather, it indicates the Lord’s ever-fresh, indescribable power to enchant, to surprise, and to draw the heart into wonder at every moment. She then locates this māyan as the mannu vaḍa-madurai maindan—the eternally established Prince of the northern Madhurā.

This “Madhurā” is not a casual distortion but a more ancient form preserved in Tamil. Madhu means nectar or honey, and madhurā is “that city whose very streets flow with honey,” a poetic expression for its sweetness and charm. Tradition speaks of two Madhurās: the southern Madhurā (Madurai) of Mīnākṣī and Sundareshvara, and the northern Madhurā, the Mathurā of Vraja-līlā where Krishna manifested His childhood sports. It is to this northern Madhurā that Āṇḍāḷ points when she says vaḍa-madurai maindanai—the divine son of that northern city.

She next invokes Him as tūya perunīr yamunai ttuṟaivan—the Lord who dwells on the pure, sacred banks of the great river Yamunā. Here, Āṇḍāḷ subtly contrasts Yamunā’s bold, fearless sevā with Godāvarī’s hesitation in Rāmāvatāra. When Sītā was abducted, she is said to have appealed to Godāvarī to bear witness and convey the truth to Rāma, yet the river, though endowed with the power to “speak” through her course and flow, remained silent, out of fear of Rāvaṇa as suggested by many Āchāryas. By contrast, Yamunā does not shrink from danger. Knowing well that if she allows Krishna to cross from Madhurā to Gokulam, the tyrant Kaṁsa may punish her or dam her flow, she still gladly offers herself as the Lord’s pathway. In doing so, she becomes tūya, surpassingly pure, and shines as the river that chooses the Lord’s service over her own safety.

Āṇḍāḷ then describes Krishna as the aṇi viḷakku—the resplendent lamp—who appears amidst the Āyar clan, illuminating the entire kulam by His mere presence. The Vraja-vāsis, the cowherds, and even the Yādavas and Vṛṣṇis may not have received from the world the honour they truly deserved, yet by having Him as their maindan, their son and kinsman, they are transformed into a luminous community, like a dark house suddenly lit by a bright lamp. That same lamp shines in the inner chamber of Yaśodā, where the Lord of all lokas stands bent and submissive, letting Himself be tied by the rope of a village-mother’s love.

It is this Lord that Āṇḍāḷ hails as tāyai kkuḍal viḷakkam śeyda Dāmōdaran, He who became the lamp of Yaśodā’s womb, the child who filled a simple cowherdess’s life with light and meaning. The One who created and sustains all universes now appears as a small boy, apparently dependent and obedient to an unlettered village mother. This dāmōdara-līlā is not a mere sweet story; it is the revelation of the Lord’s sauśīlya and saulabhya, His readiness to bend, to be bound, to belong to His devotee. What then are Āṇḍāḷ and her sakhīs to do before such a Lord? She says that they will come tūyōmāy, having purified themselves through the prior pāsurams, and will offer tū malar (pure flowers), worshipping Him by tūmalar tūvi ttoḻudu, doing arcana with all the varieties of blossoms they have gathered. Their worship is not merely external. They will praise Him vāyināl pāḍi, with the tongue, through nāma-saṅkīrtana, and manattināl śindikka, by contemplating Him deeply with the mind. Body, speech, and mind are all enlisted into bhagavad-ārādhana, turning the simple act of flower-offering into a holistic upāsanā.

Finally, Āṇḍāḷ gives one of the most compassionate assurances in the entire Tiruppāvai: the pōya piḻaiyum, pugudaruvān ninṟanavum, all sins of the past, whatever might still arise in the future, and even those that cling and stand with us now, will be burnt up like a heap of cotton thrown into blazing fire. Just as dry cotton cannot remain when touched by fire, the pāpas accumulated over countless births cannot endure when one truly engages in bhagavad-anusmaraṇa, nāma-saṅkīrtana, and takes refuge in Him through bhakti-yoga or nyāsa-yoga (śaraṇāgati). Āchāryas draw here on Upaniṣadic images in which sins are said to be destroyed in the fire of knowledge and devotion, pointing out that this verse quietly contains the seed of mokṣa-sādhana.

Walking in her footsteps, Bhagavad Rāmānuja later unfolded this very tattva–hita–puruṣārtha structure in his Śrī Bhāṣya, clarifying the truth of the Lord (tattva), the means (hita) in the form of bhakti and prapatti, and the ultimate goal of mokṣa (puruṣārtha). Āṇḍāḷ, through this single pāsuram, sows the living seed of bhakti in the heart, showing that approaching Dāmōdara with pure flowers, pure speech, and pure thought is not a small village vow, but the royal path that carries the soul towards liberation in Vishṇu’s eternal abode.
