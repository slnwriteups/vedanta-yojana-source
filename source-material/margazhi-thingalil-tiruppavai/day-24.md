# Day 24: Anṟivvulagam Aḷandāy

@verse 24

Āṇḍāl strings together six "pōṟṟi"-s, each tied to a famous act of protection. She first praises the Vāmana–Trivikrama feat, "anṟivvulagam aḷandāy aḍi pōṟṟi" – glory to Your feet that measured all the worlds. Then "shenṟaṅgu tten ilaṅgai sheṟṟāy tiṟal pōṟṟi" – glory to the might with which You went south to Laṅkā and destroyed Rāvaṇa. "ponṟa ccagaḍam udaittāy pugaḻ pōṟṟi" praises the baby’s kick that smashed the cart-demon Shakaṭāsura, while "kanṟu kuṇilā eṟindāy kaḻal pōṟṟi" recalls the playful yet deadly hurling of the calf-demon at the tree-demon. "kunṟu kuḍaiyāy eḍuttāy kuṇam pōṟṟi" blesses the goodness shown when He lifted Govardhana like a parasol. Finally, "venṟu pagai keḍukkum nin kaiyil vēl pōṟṟi" turns from the feet to the hand that wields the spear, praising the Lord whose weapon destroys enmity itself.

In all these, the focus is not on abstract divinity but on moving limbs—feet that stride, kick, lift, fling; a hand that protects. Āṇḍāl is watching the Lord walk from His private chamber to the throne, and each remembered līlā becomes a way of savouring His current gait.

The inner reflection grows out of this physicality. Devotees often delight not only in what the Lord did but in how He moves—the gait that is uniquely His. Āṇḍāl hints that the gopīs urge Krishna to seat Himself on the throne not just to submit a petition but to relish every step He takes on the way there, seeing in each step an echo of Vāmana’s stride, Govardhana’s lifting, or the child’s playful kicks. His walk from chamber to simhāsana becomes a compact re-enactment of His cosmic and childish victories.

Only after filling their minds with this vision do they speak their purpose openly: "enṟenṟun shēvagamē ētti ppaṟai koḷvān inṟu yām vandōm" – we have come today to ask for paṟai on one condition, that forever our occupation is Your service alone. The in-depth message aligns with this: Āṇḍāl uses the external theme of demon-slaying and royal walking to point to a deeper "slaying" of inner enemies—ego, forgetfulness, and self-ownership—so that the soul can stand as a humble servant, watching each step of the Lord with overflowing joy and crying "pōṟṟi" from that place of surrendered delight.
