# Day 18: Undu Mada Kaḷiṟṟan

@verse 18

Āṇḍāl first paints a vivid picture of early morning outside Nandagopa’s house. Powerful elephants, their foreheads flowing with intoxicating madu (musth), roam the courtyard. Bees abandon flower nectar to hover around this irresistible fragrance. These elephants belong to Nandagopa, whose wealth and strength are also shown through the valiant wrestlers (tōḷ valiyan) and guards he employs. Addressing Nappinnai as marumagaḷē (daughter-in-law), Āṇḍāl praises her natural fragrance (kandam kamaḻum kuḻali) that needs no flowers. Dawn signs surround the house: cocks crowing everywhere (vandeṅgum kōḻi), and cuckoos singing from mādhavī creepers draped over sandalwood pandals. This rich sensory world—elephant rut, bird calls, creeper fragrance—creates an auspicious atmosphere for bhakti.

Āṇḍāl then makes her intimate request: "pandār virali," you whose fingers hold the play-ball, come open the door so we may sing your beloved’s name (un maittunan pēr pāḍa). Use your lotus-like hands (shendāmarai kkaiyāl) to lift the latch, letting bracelets chime as you joyfully receive us. Nappinnai represents Lakshmī, the purushakāra-bhūtā who recommends devotees to Vishṇu. On the inner level, this is the soul’s prayer to the divine consort for access to the Lord. The pāsuram holds special significance in Sri Vaishṇava tradition: it is the verse with which Rāmānuja is remembered to have swooned in ecstasy when Attuzhāy, the daughter of his āchārya, opened the door of her house with jingling bangles to offer him bhikshā, for in her he saw Nappinnai herself.
