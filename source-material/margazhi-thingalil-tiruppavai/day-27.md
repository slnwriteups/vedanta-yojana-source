# Day 27: Kūḍārai Vellum

@verse 27

Āṇḍāl addresses Krishna as “kūḍārai vellum shīr kōvindā” – the glorious Lord who conquers even enemies’ hearts through grace. The gopīs declare they have received the highest honour: singing His praise and obtaining paṟai. Now comes the celebration of breaking the fast. They will adorn themselves completely: shūḍagamē (bangles), tōḷ vaḷaiyē (shoulder ornaments), tōḍē (earrings), shevi ppūvē (ear flowers), pāḍagamē (anklets), and countless other jewels. Then, dressed in new clothes provided by Krishna, they will eat together the rich pāl shōṟu – milk rice flowing with ghee so abundant that it overflows from hand to elbow. This is no ordinary meal but sāyujyam, union with the Lord through shared feast.

The inner teaching reveals complete bhakti-reward. Krishna, who “wins hearts,” grants not mere liberation but the joy of eternal fellowship. The adornments symbolize the soul beautified by His grace – every ornament a virtue He bestows. The overflowing ghee-rice mirrors akkāra vaḍishil, the traditional sweet prepared on the 27th day of Dhanurmāsam, made with milk, rice, jaggery, cashews, raisins, cardamom – offered first as naivedyam, then shared in His company.

Āṇḍāl shows that true fast-breaking is not ending physical hunger but entering divine intimacy. The gopīs, once fasting in separation, now feast in union. Their vow completes not in solitude but at Krishna’s table, where every ornament shines with His gift and every mouthful testifies to conquered hearts and fulfilled longing.

Tirumaalirancholai (Azhagar Kōyil) perfectly embodies pāsuram 27’s sāyujyam through Kallazhagar (Sundararāja Perumāḷ) and Āṇḍāl. The temple’s daily akkāra vaḍishil naivedyam – milk rice with jaggery, ghee, cashews, raisins – exactly matches the gopīs’ pāl shōṟu overflowing from hand to elbow, symbolizing union through shared feast. Āṇḍāl’s idol depicts her offering this sweet, living the sāyujyam theme of devotees eating at the divine table.

Sāyujyam (sāyujya-mukti) literally means “complete union” or “oneness” with the divine. In Hindu philosophy, it represents one of the four main types of moksha (liberation), alongside sālokya (same world as the Lord), sāmīpya (proximity to the Lord), and sārūpya (same form as the Lord). In broader Vedānta traditions, sāyujyam suggests the jīvātman merging completely into Brahman, losing distinct identity – like a river entering the ocean. In Sri Vaishṇava bhakti (relevant to Tiruppāvai), sāyujyam transforms into bhakti-sāyujyam: not dissolution, but eternal loving service where separation ends and hearts become one in bliss. The overflowing ghee-rice feast symbolizes sāyujyam as union through shared joy – devotees adorned with Krishna’s gifts, eating at His table.

Āṇḍāl herself seems to point to this feast for the Lord of Tirumaalirancholai, of whom she sings elsewhere, “nūṟu taḍā niṟainda akkāra aḍishil sonnēn” – I have vowed a hundred vessels brimming with akkāra aḍishil. Kallazhagar embodies the “kūḍārai vellum” Krishna; the hundred vessels mirror the gopīs’ feast; and Mārgazhi pilgrims who offer akkāra vaḍishil on this day live Āṇḍāl’s vision.
