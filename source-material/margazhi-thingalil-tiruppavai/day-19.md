# Day 19: Kuttu Viḷakkeriya

@verse 19

Āṇḍāl describes a lamp-lit room where festival lamps (kuttu viḷakku) burn brightly, revealing an ivory bed with carved legs (kōṭṭukkāl kaṭṭil), made from the tusks of elephants that Nandagopa’s house proudly owns. Upon this bed is spread a soft, special mattress called pañja shayanam, said to possess five qualities: it is beautiful, pleasant, soft, fragrant and bright, echoing the qualities of Ādishesha who usually serves as His couch. On this bed, it is not Krishna alone who reclines. He is lying with His head resting on the bosom of Nappinnai, whose flower-decked tresses have shed petals all over the bed, silently testifying to the loving play and closeness the couple have enjoyed through the night. Āṇḍāl lovingly calls Him “malar mārbā,” the one with the lotus-like chest where Sri resides, and pleads, “vāy tiṟavāy,” open your mouth, implying “wake up, speak, respond to us.”

Then Āṇḍāl addresses Nappinnai directly as the one with dark, broad, collyrium-lined eyes (mai ttaḍam kaṇṇināy), gently chiding her: how long will you refuse to let your husband rise, how long will you keep Him only to yourself. She points out that if Nappinnai cannot bear even a moment’s separation from Krishna, so too the devotees outside cannot bear separation, and if she insists on clinging to Him exclusively, that is “not tattvam,” not in harmony with true doctrine and compassion. Underneath the playful tone lies a firm theological insight: paramātman (Krishna) is eternally distinct from the jīvātman yet is the inner soul of all; Sri (here as Nappinnai) is not a rival claimant but the merciful mediator who shares Him with the many. Because of this exquisite blend of intimate imagery and profound tattva, later Āchāryas such as Parāshara Bhaṭṭar chose this verse as the model for the Sanskrit opening “nīḷātuṅga-stana-giri,” treating it almost as a template, and the 19th pāsuram became especially cherished in the Sri Vaishṇava tradition.
