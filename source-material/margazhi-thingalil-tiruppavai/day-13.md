# Day 13: Puḷḷin Vāy Kīṇḍānai

@verse 13

Āṇḍāḷ now calls to a girl famed for her beautiful, flower-like eyes, urging her to join the group by singing of the Lord who tore open the beak of the bird, puḷḷin vāy kīṇḍānai: Kaṇṇan, who slew Bakāsura, the demon Kaṁsa sent in the guise of a crane. For a companion whose heart belongs to Śrī Rāma, she adds pollā arakkanaik kiḷḷik kaḷaindānai, Rāma who plucked away the heads of the wicked rākṣasa Rāvaṇa, continuing the praise from the previous pāsuram; to Āṇḍāḷ, Rāma and Krishna are one and the same. Just as the 6th pāsuram used the chirping of birds and conch sounds from the temple to awaken sleepy friends, here the 13th echoes that natural imagery with birds awakening at dawn, signaling the time for vrata observance has come. The gopīs sing glories of the Lord while most friends, tired of repeated wakings, have already gathered at the kaḷam, leaving only Āṇḍāḷ persisting outside. This verse deepens the allegory: the birds represent the soul’s innate call to devotion, much like the 6th pāsuram’s leader bird guiding the flock, but now the focus shifts to celestial signs. Veḷḷi eḻundu viyāḻam uṟaṅgiṟṟu describes an astronomical event where Venus rises as Jupiter sets, marking an auspicious dawn perfect for bhakti, paralleling the 6th’s temple sounds as divine invitations to awaken from spiritual slumber.

The girl inside questions the urgency, so Āṇḍāḷ counters her hesitation: the birds are already chirping, puḷḷum śilambina kāṇ, so do not waste those lovely eyes, pōdari kkaṇṇināy, by sleeping longer. She paints the scene of the first invigorating dip in the cold waters of Tirumukkuḷam pond, where initial chill gives way to joyful immersion, symbolizing surrender to divine grace. Do not lie on in your bed, paḷḷi kkiḍattiyō, savouring the memory of Krishna all by yourself; kaḷḷam tavirndu kalandēlōr, give up this stealth and join us, emphasizing community over isolation in bhakti. What is sweet is not to be enjoyed alone but shared with the Lord’s devotees, and some commentators connect these words with Śrī Rāmānuja, who did not keep to himself the secret teaching he received from Tirukkōṭṭiyūr Nambi but opened it to all. Compared to the 6th pāsuram’s broad awakening call through natural and temple sounds, the 13th personalizes it with astronomical precision, personal beauty, and group urgency, teaching progressive devotion. The asura’s defeat mirrors Rāma’s protective valor from pāsuram 12, while the cold bath evokes shared austerity. Ultimately, Āṇḍāḷ dismantles excuses, revealing today’s perfection for praising the Lord who conquers evil, inviting every soul into the collective ecstasy of nāyaki bhāva.
